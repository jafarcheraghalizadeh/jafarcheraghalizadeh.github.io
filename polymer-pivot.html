Chain-size scaling: the exponent ν

Green markers: sampled root-mean-square distance from the first bead versus contour distance s. Dashed gold: s3/4 slope reference, normalized to a measured midpoint. This reference illustrates the exponent; its amplitude is not an independent prediction.

The RMS plot has expected asymptotic slope ν = 0.75; plotting squared distance instead would give slope 2ν = 1.5. Finite chain length, warm-up, and correlated samples affect the measured shape.

What does theory predict?

For a long self-avoiding chain in two dimensions, the equilibrium size scales as ⟨R²⟩ ≈ A N3/2, with ν = 3/4. The amplitude A depends on the lattice model, and finite chains have corrections. This is a chain-length scaling law, not an MSD-versus-time law. Read the simulation study ↗

For a stationary anchored chain, endpoint and center-of-mass MSD can saturate at long Monte Carlo lags as configurations decorrelate. A logarithmic scale does not remove this physical property of the sampled configurations. ν characterizes equilibrium size versus contour length, not the time dependence of pivot moves.

Pivot moves are nonlocal Monte Carlo updates. Their sweep count is algorithmic sampling time; no Brownian or Rouse diffusion law is implied. Increase the sample count and repeat runs before drawing conclusions.

How a pivot works

  1. Choose a pivot bead uniformly among beads 0 to N − 1.
  2. Apply one of the seven nonidentity square-lattice rotations or reflections to the tail.
  3. Reject the proposal if any beads overlap; otherwise accept it. All allowed chains have the same weight in this athermal model.

The chain has N bonds and N + 1 beads. Reset or changing chain length starts a straight chain and clears statistics. Changing sweep interval preserves the configuration and samples.

pivot = random.randrange(N)
transform = random.choice(lattice_symmetries)
proposal = transform_tail(chain, pivot, transform)
